THE PINK DOOR
— three ways to build it

Same premise, same door, same monkeys, same ending image. Three genuinely different setups — different point of view, different production size, different juror fighting for it in the room. Pick the trade, not the pitch.

Prepared 2026-08-12 Deadline Sep 3, 11:59pm PT · 22 days Recommendation Option C

Built on Dan's notes. The escalation ladder (phones out → shopkeepers and security → animal control with nets), the closing door as a clock, and the hesitant monkey the boy has to convince to jump — all Dan's, all adopted into every option below. His beat sheet's best idea, the closest monkey who never came through and waits by the door, became the spine of Option A and a cutaway in the other two.

Locked in all three — don't re-litigate these

Already agreed in the channel, and each one removes a way to lose:

  • Wordless. No lipsync risk, no language barrier for a global jury, and the sound design carries — our strongest criterion.
  • Stylized, not photoreal. Catmull's first language, and it sidesteps the uncanny valley that sinks half the field.
  • One style, worlds separated by palette and light — not three different art styles. Three styles reads as craft on display, which is the one thing all three judges reject.
  • Three named monkeys with distinct silhouettes, one blessed reference each. A troop is an unbounded cast.
  • Somebody pays something. Non-negotiable — it is Catmull's primary rejection criterion.
  • ≥3:00 runtime (target 3:20), and a real master shot for any sequence with more than two characters in it.

And the rules bind all three. Every frame generated on higgsfield.ai, from scratch, inside Aug 10 → Sep 3. Audio is the only exemption — make it anywhere, but upload every file into the submission project. 16:9 or 21:9. Never clean up the project. The gate-by-gate read on each option is below, and it is the thing that actually separates them.

A

THE WAITING ONE

Told from the jungle side · the monkey who stayed

We never follow the boy. We stay with the one monkey who didn't jump. He watches his friend and his family vanish through a door, and then he waits beside it. He brings fruit and sets it down. He sleeps against the frame. Rain comes. The door dims. Everything happening in the city reaches us only as what's visible through the doorway — a rectangle of another world, glimpsed and misunderstood.

The cost: the door is closing and he could step through and be with them. He doesn't. He stays on the side where home is, so there is still a home to come back to. When they finally spill back through, he is the reason the door was still open.

The trade
Wins
  • By far the smallest build on the slate — one location, one character, the city as a handful of controlled plates inside a frame you own
  • Consistency stops being the enemy: almost nothing can drift
  • The most emotionally direct film of the three
  • Finishable with a week to spare, which nothing else here is
Risks
  • Throws away the chase, the reveal, and Anderson — our only committed advocate
  • Collides with KEEPER and QUIET, which already own the Catmull and Papamichael lanes
  • "Beautiful but small" is a real failure mode in a field of spectacle
  • Needs the single best sound design of the three to survive

Build size · small

B

THE CLOSING DOOR

Told from the boy's side · Dan's structure, disciplined

The straight-ahead version, and the closest to what's already written. Boy's point of view, linear. He convinces the hesitant monkey to jump. City. No door on this side — stranded. The escalation ladder runs: phones out, then shopkeepers, then animal control. The hesitant one is taken, and he watches the truck drive away. The door is dimming. He chooses to go after the truck anyway.

The reveal still lands where it always did: while he was chasing, the others were building. He gave up his way home for a friend, and his friends had been making him a new one the whole time. No crystal, no MacGuffin — the pieces are the point.

Build size · large

C

THE BUILD

Told from the monkeys' side · a heist

Flip the protagonist. The monkeys are the leads and the boy is what they're saving. They follow him through, they see him stranded and frightened in a world he can't read — and they decide. They case the city. They pick targets. They steal specific things. They build.

The reveal becomes the premise, and that is an upgrade. We know from minute one what they're doing. The boy doesn't. So we watch him think his friends have abandoned him for shiny objects, watch him be ashamed of them, maybe try to stop them — while we can see they are building him a way home. That's not a twist you wait for; it's dramatic irony, and it plays for three straight minutes instead of thirty seconds.

The cost: the last piece is out of reach, and the hesitant one — the one who was scared of the water, who almost didn't come — goes for it and is taken. The door opens without him. The boy, who spent the whole film misreading them, is the one who turns around and goes back.

Who fights for it
Catmull

Non-human characters with a want, a plan and a sacrifice. He invented this in 1986.

Papamichael

A heist demands readable space by design — so the geography has to hold, and he'll see that it does.

Anderson

Physical objective, time pressure, thresholds, a plan you can follow. Native grammar.

The trade
Wins
  • The only option all three judges would argue for
  • A heist self-limits the build — you pick 3–4 target locations because the plan needs them, not because the montage wants them
  • Wordless is native: heists are pure visual logic
  • Gives each of the three monkeys a job, so they become characters instead of copies
  • Dramatic irony beats a late twist, and costs nothing to shoot
Risks
  • The most new writing — a heist needs a plan the audience can follow, and that's real script work
  • Loses the surprise of the reveal (traded for three minutes of irony — worth it, but it is a trade)
  • The boy must stay sympathetic while misjudging his friends
  • Still shares the Anderson lane with BREACH

Build size · medium

Against the nine gates

The rules separate these three more than the craft does

Stage 1 is pure eligibility — no judge sees the film until it clears every gate. Graded honestly, one of these three is fragile in a way that has nothing to do with whether it's a good story.

Gate 07 · still unanswered

Every one of these has a boy in it, and nobody has read the clause yet

Gate 07 lists "no sexual content, no character depicted under 18." Almost certainly an anti-sexualisation rule. But it is still unread, and I am not going to recommend a setup that bets three weeks on the charitable interpretation without saying so.

So the real test is: which option survives if the clause turns out to be literal? That question separates them cleanly — and it is the strongest argument in the recommendation.

Recommendation

Option C — and steal Option A's best shot for the cutaway

C is the only one of the three where every juror has something to fight for, and it is the only one where the production discipline comes from the story itself: a heist can't sprawl, because the audience has to be able to follow the plan. That constraint is doing for free what we'd otherwise have to impose by hand.

It also fixes the deepest problem in the original — the passive protagonist — not by giving the boy more to do, but by admitting who the story was always about. The monkeys had the want, the plan and the agency from the first draft. This just stops pretending otherwise.

Then take the one image from A that's too good to lose: the monkey on the jungle side, sitting by the door, setting down fruit, sleeping against the frame. Three or four cutaways, one location, one character — the cheapest generations in the film, and they give it its whole emotional floor while the heist runs. That's Dan's beat sheet earning its keep.

And the rules agree with the craft here, which is rare. C is one of the two options that survives gate 07 read literally — the leads are monkeys, so if the clause turns out to bar a child on screen, the boy becomes a young adult and not one beat changes. B is the option that dies to that reading, and it is also the option most likely to run out of days. Picking C means we are not betting three weeks on a clause nobody has read yet.
